Learn 3D with the Who's Good AU Box

three.js is a free JavaScript library that draws 3D pictures in your web browser. Games, product viewers and fancy websites use it. Here you learn the seven big ideas, and every idea has a live 3D scene you can play with. The shapes are the Who's Good AU box: a rounded box with the brand gradient (yellow at the foot, blue at the head) and the white question mark.

Lesson 1

The three ingredients

Every three.js picture needs the same three things. Think of a theatre:

SceneThe stage. Everything you want to show goes onto it.
CameraYour eyes. It decides where you look from.
RendererThe painter. It paints what the camera sees onto the screen.
drag to look around

Press both buttons and watch what changes. When the painter stops, the picture freezes, even though the box is still turning inside the computer.

const scene    = new THREE.Scene();                    // the stage
const camera   = new THREE.PerspectiveCamera(50, width / height);  // the eyes
const renderer = new THREE.WebGLRenderer();            // the painter

scene.add(box);                   // put the box on the stage
renderer.render(scene, camera);   // paint one picture

Quick check: which one paints the picture onto your screen?

Lesson 2

Shapes: geometry + material = mesh

A 3D object is called a mesh. It is made of two parts: the geometry is the shape (a rounded box, a ball…), and the material is the skin (its color and how shiny it is). Every shape is secretly built from tiny flat triangles. Turn on "wireframe" to see them.

drag to look around

White keeps the brand gradient. Any other color is mixed into it.

Try this: pick the sphere, turn on wireframe, and count roughly how many triangles go around the middle.

Lesson 3

Moving things: position, rotation, scale

3D space has three directions, called axes: x goes left–right (red line), y goes up–down (green line), z goes towards you and away (blue line). Every object has a position (where), a rotation (turned how far) and a scale (how big).

drag to look around
Good to know: three.js measures turning in radians, not degrees. A full turn (360°) is 2 × π ≈ 6.28 radians. Watch the number in the code change as you move the rotation slider.

Quick check: which axis points up?

Lesson 4

Light and shadow

Without light, a 3D world is pitch black, just like a room at night. Some materials react to light and some ignore it. The little yellow ball is the light bulb. Ambient light is soft light that comes from everywhere, like a cloudy sky.

drag to look around
Try this: set ambient light to 0 and move the bulb behind the box. Then switch to MeshBasicMaterial. What happens?

Lesson 5

Animation: many pictures per second

Movement on a screen is a trick, just like in a cartoon flip-book. The computer paints a still picture, changes things a tiny bit, and paints again, about 60 times every second. This repeating is called the animation loop.

drag to look around

The counter in the corner shows how many pictures were painted so far.

function animate() {
  box.rotation.y += speed * delta;            // turn a tiny bit
  ball.position.y = Math.abs(Math.sin(time)); // bounce up and down
  renderer.render(scene, camera);             // paint one picture
  requestAnimationFrame(animate);             // ...and do it all again
}
animate();
Try this: tick "slow motion". Now you can see the single pictures, like a flip-book.

Quick check: why does the box seem to move?

Lesson 6

The camera and perspective

The camera has a field of view (short: fov): how wide it can see, in degrees. A small number is like a zoom lens (you see less, but bigger). A big number is like a fish-eye lens. Things far away look smaller: that is called perspective.

drag to look around

Your eye sees about 120° from side to side, but games mostly use 60–90°.

Lesson 7

Putting it together: a solar system

Objects can carry other objects, like a parent carrying a child. In three.js this is a Group. The Moon sits inside the Earth's group, so when the Earth travels around the Sun, the Moon comes along, and it still circles the Earth at the same time. This is called a scene graph (a family tree of objects).

drag to look around
scene
 └─ sun
 └─ earthOrbit (Group)
     └─ earth
         └─ moonOrbit (Group)
             └─ moon
const earthOrbit = new THREE.Group();   // an invisible "arm" from the Sun
scene.add(earthOrbit);
earth.position.x = 3.2;                 // put Earth at the end of the arm
earthOrbit.add(earth);                  // Earth is a child of the arm
earth.add(moonOrbit);                   // the Moon's arm is a child of Earth

// in the animation loop: just turn the arms
earthOrbit.rotation.y += 0.3 * delta;
moonOrbit.rotation.y  += 2.0 * delta;

Quick check: when the Earth's group turns, what happens to the Moon?
